## 4. ROTEIRO — narração integral

Hook, termina na pergunta do título:

In the wrecked inside of Sahure's pyramid, an antechamber had almost vanished. Only one corner survived, and about thirty centimetres of its eastern wall. Yet in that stub of stone, a team found the trace of a low passage that an English explorer sketched in 1836, and that the standard modern plan seems to leave out. Beyond it lay storerooms that, according to the reports, earlier documentation had not recorded. A handheld laser scanner went in and measured them. So what were eight rooms doing beyond a passage that history had lost?

If you follow evidence into places no one was meant to see, subscribe — now, let's see what the scan recorded.

Here is what makes the record strange. These rooms are not intact. According to the mission's announcement, the northern and southern parts of the storerooms are badly damaged, ceiling and original floor included. What survives is remnants of the original walls and patches of the old floor. So the scan is not a picture of a sealed treasury. It is a measurement of what a collapse left behind.

And the mission says it documented the layout and dimensions of every one of those rooms. I have to be honest with you: the public reports I found give no figures. We know the rooms were measured. We do not yet know the numbers.

That is the first thing to hold on to. The scan is a record of damage, and the damage is the story. Rooms that are broken are rooms that went unseen for a long time, and that raises the question that bothered me first. How does a pyramid that has been explored since the early nineteenth century still have eight rooms that nobody had properly recorded?

The answer starts with one low passage, and with why anyone doubted it was real.

The doubt begins with a drawing. John Shae Perring was an English engineer who surveyed pyramids in the 1830s. In 1836, he put a low passage on his plan of Sahure's inner rooms, a narrow opening in an eastern wall. Accounts differ on whether he placed it in the antechamber or in the burial chamber, and I will come back to why that matters.

For the next century and a half, that opening was a line on paper and nothing more. Rubble had swallowed it. Nobody could walk to it, point at it, and say, here.

Perring found something else down there, too: a fragment of black basalt that he took to be from the king's sarcophagus. Current accounts describe that fragment as the only trace of the burial ever found. Think about how little that is. Until 2023, the best-documented object inside the pyramid of a king was a broken piece of stone, and the best-documented doorway was one that nobody could find again.

So a nineteenth-century explorer drew a passage. Why would the twentieth century not follow it?

The next serious attempt came from Ludwig Borchardt, the German architect and Egyptologist whose expedition dug at Abusir between 1907 and 1908. His publication of Sahure's complex, finished in the years that followed, became the standard reference, and it still is. And here the story forks.

Some later critics argue that Borchardt's plan left out Perring's low passage. They also argue that he leaned on the layouts of two later Abusir pyramids, those of Neferirkare and Niuserre, to fill gaps in a ruined interior. I want to be careful here. I have this from critiques of his work, not from the 1910 volume itself. Treat it as an attributed argument, not a verdict on a man who worked in a wrecked pyramid with the tools of his day.

But notice the consequence, because it is simple. If the standard plan skipped the passage, then nobody was looking beyond it. A line missing from the map is also a door nobody tries to open.

Which leaves another question. Why was this interior so hard to read in the first place?

Because Sahure's pyramid had been taken apart. And it helps to know what was taken apart. Sahure was the second king of Egypt's Fifth Dynasty, ruling some four and a half thousand years ago, give or take the usual uncertainty of Old Kingdom dates. He chose Abusir for his burial, a plateau between Giza and Saqqara, and his pyramid was the first royal monument there. Kings who followed him copied the pattern: a smaller pyramid, a richly decorated temple, and a long causeway down to the valley. Sahure's causeway is reported at about two hundred and thirty-five metres. So this was not a minor tomb. It was the template.

When it was new, it stood about forty-seven metres high, roughly a hundred and fifty-four feet, on a base of about seventy-eight metres. Its core was not the huge fitted blocks of Giza. It was smaller, roughly cut local limestone, laid in layers around a central axis and bound with debris and mortar, then wrapped in fine casing stone.

That design has a weakness. When the casing was stripped away for reuse, the rubble heart was exposed to weather. Stone robbers tunnelled in. Ceilings gave way. Debris poured down the corridors and filled the rooms. By the time Perring arrived, the inside was already a wreck. By the time anyone wanted to stabilise it, the wreck was unstable.

And that is exactly where the modern story begins, not with a search for rooms, but with a plan to keep a ceiling from falling.

In 2019, an Egyptian-German mission began work on the substructure. It is described as being led from the University of Würzburg by the Egyptologist Mohamed Ismail Khaled, and supported by the American Research Center in Egypt through its Antiquities Endowment Fund. Its stated goal was conservation: stabilise the inner rooms, and make the burial chambers safe to enter.

Think about what that means. You cannot prop up a ceiling you cannot see. You cannot rebuild a wall if you do not know where it stood. So the first job was never discovery. It was measurement.

That brings us back to the antechamber, and to those thirty centimetres of wall. How do you recover a room from one corner and a foot of stone?

The reports do not explain the method, and I will not invent one. What they do say is that the team worked out the original dimensions and floor plan of the antechamber, and replaced the destroyed walls with new retaining walls. My own reading, and I flag it as inference, is that a corner and a stretch of wall give you two things: a direction and a thickness. If the builders were consistent, and in a royal pyramid they usually were, that is enough to begin.

And in the eastern wall, traces of a low passage appeared. The same passage Perring had drawn. The reports say that excavating it opened the way to the storerooms.

Now, the machine.

Trade coverage says the team worked with 3D Geoscan, a surveying company in Alexandria, and used a ZEB Horizon, a handheld scanner made by GeoSLAM. Its reported figures are about three hundred thousand points per second and a relative accuracy of roughly six millimetres, about the thickness of a pencil. One caution: news reports of the mission describe 3D laser scanning in general, and the model name comes from trade press. So I treat that specification as reported, not confirmed.

How does a device like that work inside a pyramid? A laser sends out light and times how long it takes to come back. Do that hundreds of thousands of times a second, in every direction, and you collect a cloud of points, each one a position in space.

The clever part is the walking. The scanner compares each new view with what it has already recorded, and works out how it has moved. It builds the map and finds itself on the map at once. That technique is called simultaneous localisation and mapping, and it matters here for a plain reason: there is no GPS under forty-seven metres of limestone.

A tripod scanner would need to be set up, moved, and aligned again and again, in a narrow, unstable passage. A handheld one lets an operator walk the route and walk out with a three-dimensional record.

But a point cloud has hard limits. It measures surfaces. It tells you where stone is, and where stone is not. It does not date a wall. It cannot tell you whether a block is original or a modern repair. It cannot say what a room was for. That is the work of archaeologists reading the cloud against the masonry, the joints, the tool marks, and the find spots.

So the scan can answer how big. It cannot, by itself, answer what for.

There is a second, quieter use, and it may matter more than any single room. A cloud like this can be sliced. An archaeologist can cut a section through a wall, lay the result over an old drawing, and see exactly where the drawing and the stone disagree. That is how a missing passage shows up: not as a discovery, but as a gap between two records. The published reports do not say the team did this, so take it as how the tool is normally used, not as what happened here.

And it protects something. Every damaged ceiling and broken wall in that cloud is a piece of the pyramid that may not stand next year. A measurement made now is a record made before the next collapse.

Which brings me to the question I keep returning to. Did the scanner find these rooms?

Trade headlines say it helped reveal them. The mission's own announcement says something different: that the team excavated the lost passage, and that this opened the way to the storerooms. Both statements can be true. A scanner can help a team understand a space it has just opened. But I cannot separate the two from the public sources, and I would rather say that than hand a machine credit it has not been shown to earn.

I started out thinking this was a story about a scanner. I now think it is a story about a door. The digging found the door. The scan made what lay behind it measurable.

That is a quieter story than the headlines. It is also, I think, a truer one.

It matters because of how these stories travel. A press release says a team documented eight storerooms. A headline turns that into secret rooms found by a scanner. By the time it reaches a thumbnail, it is a hidden chamber waiting to be opened. Each step sounds like a small rounding. Together, they turn a conservation project into a treasure hunt, and they hide the part that is actually remarkable: that careful, patient work reopened a door that was drawn on paper nearly two hundred years ago.

If you take one habit from this video, make it this one: ask who did what, with which tool, and what the source actually says.

And it makes room for an idea that a lot of people bring to the word "hidden."

The idea goes like this. These rooms sit beside the burial chamber, the most protected part of the pyramid. Kings were buried with enormous wealth. And the only object ever recovered from the burial chamber is a fragment of stone. So a side complex that history lost could be a vault no robber reached, or a clue to something about the burial that the textbooks missed.

I want to give that idea its best case, because it is a fair argument. Here is the best case: absence of recovered treasure is not proof that no treasure existed, and a sealed space is exactly where an undisturbed deposit would survive.

Now let's test it the same way we would test anything else.

First, access. A secret passage is one that nobody knew about. This one was drawn by an English engineer in 1836. At least one explorer saw it nearly two centuries ago. It was lost to rubble, not hidden by design.

Second, condition. The reports describe the storerooms as damaged, with broken ceilings and floors. That is what you would expect from a collapse and from centuries of stone robbing. It is not what you would expect from an undisturbed seal.

Third, the vocabulary. The mission calls these rooms storerooms. In archaeology, a magazine is a service space. The word treasury does not appear in any report I found, and neither does a list of objects recovered from inside.

None of that proves the rooms were never rich. It means that nothing in the public record shows a vault. The evidence that could would be a list of finds, and none has been published. So the popular idea survives only as a question, and the evidence we have favours a plainer answer.

Which leaves the plainer answer to explain. Why would a king need storerooms inside his own pyramid at all?

The reports say the rooms likely held burial goods. That fits what we know of Egyptian belief: the dead king was expected to live on, and he needed provisions, equipment, and offerings to do so. And Sahure was a king who invested heavily in being remembered. His mortuary temple carried about three hundred and seventy running metres of relief decoration, by one enduring comparison more than three times the figure reported for the temple of Khufu.

Storage at the heart of the pyramid would fit that care.

But "would fit" is not "was." Until the mission publishes the dimensions, the finds, and the architectural context, the function of these rooms is an inference. A good one. Still an inference.

So what would settle it? I can think of three things, and each is something a team can actually go and check.

One: finds. Pottery, stone vessels, or even scraps of them, lying in the rubble of a room, would say what it held. Two: marks. Storerooms in Egyptian buildings were often labelled or sealed, and a surviving mark can name a commodity or an institution. Three: comparison. If the layout echoes rooms in neighbouring pyramids, that is evidence of a standard plan; if it does not, that is evidence of something new.

Notice what is missing from that list. A treasure map. The questions that matter here are small, and they are answerable.

So, what were eight rooms doing beyond a passage that history had lost?

Here is the best answer the evidence allows. They were, in all likelihood, storerooms: service spaces for the king's burial, reached by a low passage that Perring drew in 1836, that the standard plan appears to omit, and that a team reopened while trying to keep a ruined pyramid from falling in.

The scan did not conjure them. The digging did. What the scan gave us is a measurement, accurate to a few millimetres if the reported specification holds, of rooms that were already breaking apart. In a place like this, that is not a small thing. It is a record made before the next wall moves, and a record that other researchers, who will never fit down that corridor, can open on a screen and measure for themselves.

What is established: eight rooms exist, they are damaged, and a lost passage led to them. What is possible: that they held burial goods. What is unknown: their sizes in public, what they contained, and how far the passage runs.

And I changed my mind along the way. I began with a machine. I ended with thirty centimetres of wall, a corner, and a line on a map that was drawn before anyone could use it.

There is one question left that a tape measure can settle. When the dimensions are published, will these rooms match the layout of the storerooms in Sahure's successors at Abusir, the layouts Borchardt may have borrowed, or will they show that Sahure planned something of his own?

If you want to see how that comparison turns out, subscribe — and I'll follow the measurements.
